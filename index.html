NASA EONET publishes fire detections with a few days’ delay, so a fire burning right now is usually not here yet — measured today, the most recent report anywhere was over a day old.

Coverage also changed shape. Before 2024 the catalogue is small and effectively North American; from 2024 it is global and around fifty times denser. That is a change in what was watched, not in what burned.

Magnetic axis off
The axis of the dipole that best fits Earth’s magnetic field, from the first three IGRF‑14 coefficients. Where it meets the surface are the geomagnetic poles — exactly opposite each other, and about 9° from the rotation axis.

These are not the magnetic dip poles, where a compass needle points straight down. Those come from the full field, do not sit opposite each other, and are the ones racing towards Siberia. In 2025 the geomagnetic north pole is at 80.8° N 72.8° W; the dip pole at 85.8° N 138.1° E.

Published tables quote geodetic latitude, roughly 0.06° higher than the geocentric value shown here. The figures themselves are in Almanac.

Four circles: sunset, then the end of civil, nautical and astronomical twilight — the Sun at 0°, −6°, −12° and −18° below the horizon.

The Earth in space, tilted by its real 23.4° obliquity. Meridian and Top are locked to the Earth–Sun plane, so the Earth turns beneath a Sun that stays put.

Polar motion off
The rotation axis does not sit still in the crust. It spirals around a mean position: the Chandler wobble, a free nutation with a period of about 1.19 years, plus an annual term driven by water, snow and air moving around the planet.

The scale is the whole story. The pole travels roughly 25 metres a year but never gets further than about 20 metres from home — a spiral inside a circle 40 m across. That is why this is a plot and not a mark on the globe: at maximum zoom one pixel spans several kilometres, so the whole circle is a hundredth of a pixel.

Measured, not modelled. Values before 1962 carry an uncertainty of roughly 30 mas — about a metre — so the early part of the long track is rougher than it looks.

Ocean floor on
The ocean in the standard texture is one flat colour — the Mariana Trench and the North Sea are literally the same blue. This swaps it for NASA's Blue Marble bathymetry, so ridges, trenches and shelves become visible. The land is unchanged. It replaces the day map rather than adding a layer, so it costs one download the first time you switch it on — about 0.4 MB at 2K, about 4.9 MB at 8K.
